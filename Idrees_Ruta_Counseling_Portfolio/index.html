Professional Counseling Orientation and Ethical Practice

Ethical reasoning, professional responsibilities, and decision-making in complex situations.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTEthical Case Analysis
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

The hardest part of my Ethical Case Analysis was deciding what to do with information that a 16-year-old student trusted her counselor enough to disclose. Maria reported occasional alcohol use, while her school expected the counselor to report it. I could understand the school's concern, but I also had to ask whether disclosure was legally required and what it would do to the counseling relationship. I used Corey, Corey, and Callanan's eight-step decision-making model to work through the dilemma rather than relying on my first reaction.

I kept returning to the difference between an institutional expectation and an ethical obligation. The ACA Code of Ethics, the student's welfare, confidentiality, and the limits of confidentiality all mattered. Because the case did not specify a state, I could not pretend to know the applicable state law. That uncertainty was part of the analysis. In practice, I would need to verify the law, consult with a supervisor, and document how I reached a decision.

This course also made me look more closely at counseling as a profession. Its history and philosophy help explain why client autonomy, advocacy, and the counseling relationship matter. Licensure, professional organizations, accreditation, continuing education, and supervision are not just requirements to complete. They establish what counselors are responsible for and where our competence ends. At Thresholds, I have worked alongside staff who bring different responsibilities to the same client's care. Crisis response, referrals, communication with other providers, and navigating limited resources can all affect what happens after a counseling conversation. I have seen why knowing one's role, consulting appropriately, and advocating within a system are necessary parts of helping someone.

I do not expect ethical decisions to become easy simply because I gain experience. I do expect to become better at recognizing when my judgment needs to be checked. For me, professional responsibility includes supervision, attention to my own well-being, and being willing to say when I do not know something. I would rather work through uncertainty carefully than make a confident decision I cannot defend.

CACREP CORE COMPETENCY · 02

Social and Cultural Diversity

Cultural humility, worldview, and the importance of understanding lived experience.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTCultural Immersion Final
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

For my Cultural Immersion assignment, I interviewed an African American woman about her experiences and reflected on my own assumptions. I went into the assignment knowing that culture influences how people experience the world. What I had to confront more directly was how easily knowledge about a community can turn into assumptions about an individual. Knowing a person's racial or cultural identity does not mean knowing their story.

I come from both Palestinian and Scandinavian backgrounds, with Muslim and Christian influences in my family. Growing up in Chicago also exposed me to communities whose experiences were very different from my own. I value that upbringing, but I do not see it as proof that I am culturally competent. If anything, it has made me more aware of how much I still have to ask and learn. Theories of multicultural counseling gave me language for examining identity, acculturation, worldview, and the influence of religion and family. I am especially interested in what happens when a counselor's explanation of distress differs from the client's. If faith, community obligations, or a history of discrimination shapes how someone understands their problem, I need to hear that explanation before deciding what treatment should look like.

We also examined racism, microaggressions, poverty, unequal access to services, and historical and intergenerational trauma. These are not abstract topics in community mental health. A client may be dealing with a clinical disorder and an environment that continually adds to their distress. I think it is important to recognize both rather than treating every problem as though it began inside the person.

Cultural humility means checking my own assumptions, being attentive to power in the counseling relationship, and adapting care when the client's needs call for it. Sometimes advocacy involves helping someone navigate a service they have been unable to access. Other times it begins with listening instead of assuming. I want clients to be able to tell me who they are, rather than feeling that I have already decided.

CACREP CORE COMPETENCY · 03

Human Growth and Development

Development across the lifespan and the effects of substance use.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTDevelopmental Consequences of Drug Use
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

Working with teenagers has made me pay closer attention to development. The same behavior can mean something different depending on someone's age, family relationships, and level of independence. In this course, I studied individual and family development across the lifespan, learning and personality theories, and cultural identity development. These theories helped me put behavior into a developmental context instead of judging it only by how it presents today.

For my presentation on the developmental consequences of drug use, I focused on marijuana use during adolescence. I examined how ongoing brain development relates to judgment, emotional regulation, learning, and vulnerability to substance use. This was especially relevant to my work at Thresholds, where I support teenagers with serious mental health conditions. Their age matters when considering both their symptoms and the expectations placed on them.

The course also addressed attachment, family relationships, sexual development, cultural identity, and the ways people form connections. Family structure, school, community, cultural expectations, and access to support all influence the opportunities someone has to develop and adapt. We also examined illness, disability, addiction, grief, crises, and trauma across the lifespan. I found the discussion of resilience especially useful. If I am asking how someone's experiences contributed to their symptoms, I should also ask what helped them survive those experiences. Their developmental history contains information about strengths and adaptation, not just risk factors.

I tend to think of people as the accumulation of their past experiences and earlier versions of themselves. Developmental theory gives me a more organized way to examine that idea. I want to understand how someone arrived at their present circumstances without assuming that their past limits what they can become.

CACREP CORE COMPETENCY · 04

Career Development

Career theory, personal values, changing life roles, and professional direction.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTCareer Development Final Paper
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

For my Career Development final paper, I applied Parsons' Trait and Factor Theory and Super's Life-Span, Life-Space Theory to my own career decisions. Parsons helped explain why counseling fits my interests and values. Super made more sense of how my work will change as I take on other roles in life. I do not expect the priorities I have now to be identical to the priorities I will have later in my career.

I also learned about career assessments, occupational and educational information, labor-market resources, and ways to evaluate interests, abilities, values, and working conditions. These tools can help clients compare options and make plans. However, an assessment result is not a verdict about what someone should do with their life. I questioned how easily career theories can be applied to people without much financial freedom. More often than not, people work because they have to provide for themselves or their families. A person may know exactly what career fits their interests and still be unable to pursue it because of money, disability, discrimination, or limited educational opportunities. A theory can make sense without being equally useful to everyone.

Because of this, career counseling also involves helping clients navigate real barriers. I learned about educational readiness, career decision-making, job-search and life-work skills, program planning, workplace advocacy, and ethical responsibilities. A counselor should help someone identify possibilities without ignoring the practical limits they are facing.

I have always wanted work that feels meaningful to me, which is part of why I chose counseling. At the same time, I recognize that work is not everyone's primary source of purpose. My goal is to help clients define success for themselves, whether that means pursuing a particular career, finding financial stability, or making work fit around the rest of their lives.

CACREP CORE COMPETENCY · 05

Counseling and Helping Relationships

Therapeutic presence, reflection, silence, and the difference between practicing and doing counseling.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTFinal Interview Reflection Paper
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

My Final Interview Reflection focused on a difference I keep noticing: practicing counseling is not the same as doing counseling. In a roleplay, I can get so caught up in whether I am using the right skill that I lose the natural flow of the conversation. At Thresholds, I am responding to someone who actually needs support, and that feels much more intuitive. I learned that I cannot let the pressure to perform a technique get in the way of listening.

I practiced attending, reflecting feelings, summarizing, questioning, and challenging. When I watched my recorded interview, I noticed how often I explained a question after already asking it. I was trying to make sure I was understood, but I was also taking away the client's opportunity to sit with the question. I have been learning to speak less and trust silence. Sometimes a pause does more than another sentence would.

My theory coursework introduced different ways to conceptualize a client, including existential, humanistic, and cognitive behavioral approaches. I am drawn to existential questions about meaning, identity, freedom, and responsibility, but I also see the usefulness of structured CBT interventions. I do not think every client should have to fit into the counselor's preferred theory. I have learned to connect case conceptualization with collaborative goals, measurable outcomes, culturally responsive interventions, and progress monitoring. The course also addressed documentation, telehealth, referrals, consultation, medications, suicide assessment, and crisis and trauma-informed responses. These responsibilities matter because a good relationship alone does not replace sound clinical judgment.

I now feel more comfortable allowing a session to unfold without trying to direct every moment of it. I have also learned that effective counseling does not always require an elaborate intervention. Sometimes a thoughtful reflection or an appropriate challenge can help a client examine something they had not considered before. My goal is to continue developing my clinical judgment while maintaining the genuine connection that first drew me to counseling.

CACREP CORE COMPETENCY · 06

Group Counseling and Group Work

Group design, inclusion, therapeutic factors, and intentional facilitation.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTGroup Written Proposal
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

My Group Written Proposal involved designing a sex-positive 12-step psychotherapy group for adults concerned about compulsive or dysregulated sexual behavior. The challenge was adapting a familiar recovery model without assuming that every participant had the same values or wanted abstinence. We built the proposal around individualized goals, screening, group structure, and interventions that could support people with different definitions of progress.

I studied group counseling theories, stages of group development, and therapeutic factors such as universality, cohesion, interpersonal learning, and hope. These concepts helped me understand why hearing another member's experience can sometimes be more meaningful than hearing the same idea from a therapist. The relationships between members are part of the intervention.

I also learned about screening and selecting members, setting expectations, deciding whether a group should be open or closed, and responding to conflict or unequal participation. The leader has to create enough structure for the group to function without taking over every conversation. Group type, setting, developmental level, and cultural background all affect those decisions.

My experience co-facilitating groups at Illinois Masonic has made these ideas more concrete. In virtual groups, I have seen how an activity can create a starting point for discussion, but the most useful moments often come from what members say to one another afterward. I have had to consider pacing, participation, technology, and how to make an activity therapeutic rather than simply entertaining. When I help facilitate groups, I want to be able to explain what the exercise is meant to accomplish and leave enough room for members to respond to one another. The group should not depend on me to make every connection for them.

CACREP CORE COMPETENCY · 07

Assessment and Testing

Assessment, clinical observation, and interpreting client presentation.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTAssessment Final
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

In my Assessment Final, I considered the PAI, BDI-II, MCMI-IV, and SASSI-4 for a fictional client. What stood out to me was that choosing a measure is already a clinical decision. The BDI-II can help examine depressive symptoms, while the SASSI-4 addresses substance-use concerns. Comparing these instruments helped me understand how different assessments can contribute to a fuller clinical picture.

The course required me to think about standardization, norms, reliability, and validity. These concepts are easy to list and harder to apply. A measure may be reliable without being appropriate for a particular client or purpose. I also have to consider the population used to develop it and whether language, culture, disability, or testing conditions could affect the result.

I have become more careful about the difference between a score and a clinical conclusion. Someone's responses may indicate significant symptoms, but the score does not explain their history or tell me what happened before they completed the measure. An interview, mental status examination, behavioral observations, and relevant risk screening help put the results into context. Sometimes they also raise questions the test did not address. Assessment also serves different purposes across counseling. It can inform diagnosis, identify immediate safety concerns, guide treatment planning, support educational or career decisions, and show whether symptoms are changing. That means selecting and interpreting an instrument requires appropriate training, informed consent, and attention to how the findings will actually be used.

I appreciate the structure that assessment brings to clinical work. At the same time, I do not want to confuse a standardized result with a complete understanding of a person. I see assessment as a way to test and refine my clinical thinking, including recognizing when the available information is not enough to reach a conclusion.

Research and Program Evaluation

Research design, clinical evidence, and evaluating treatment outcomes.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTResearch Proposal Final Paper
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

For my research proposal, I wanted to examine whether adolescents receiving TF-CBT through telehealth experience outcomes comparable to those receiving treatment in person. I proposed a study of 200 adolescents in Cook County over 16 weeks. I chose the question because telehealth may make treatment more accessible, but I did not want to assume that convenience and clinical effectiveness are the same thing.

I planned to measure PTSD symptoms using the CAPS-CA-5 at several points during treatment and compare the groups using statistical analysis. Writing the proposal made me think more carefully about what the data could actually show. Differences in the home environment, missed sessions, and variation between clinicians could influence outcomes even if both groups received the same treatment model. One limitation stood out: the study would end after treatment, so it could not tell us whether improvement lasted. Even a statistically significant difference would need to be interpreted in terms of its clinical importance. I learned that research findings are only as useful as the questions asked and the limits acknowledged.

We studied quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research, along with ways to evaluate counseling programs. I can see why a program needs more than attendance numbers to judge whether it is working. A needs assessment can identify who is not being reached, while ongoing and outcome evaluations can help determine whether a service should be changed. The measures also need to make sense for the population being served.

Research ethics, informed consent, confidentiality, and IRB oversight were another part of the course. I am not interested in treating research as separate from counseling. If I use an intervention, I should be able to explain the evidence behind it, where that evidence is limited, and whether it applies to the person sitting across from me.
