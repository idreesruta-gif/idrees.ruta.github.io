Treatment Planning

Connecting assessment, diagnostic impressions, goals, and interventions.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTTreatment Plan IV
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

In Treatment Plan IV, I worked with a fictional case involving a 17-year-old who was struggling with anxiety, school avoidance, marijuana use, and low self-worth. He also reported passive thoughts about death and identified his younger sister as a reason to keep trying. I could not write a useful plan by treating each of these concerns as a separate problem. The anxiety, substance use, safety concerns, and available supports all had to inform the goals.

I used the client's assessment information, strengths, risks, and presenting problems to develop long-term goals and SMART objectives. I focused on reducing anxiety, examining marijuana use, and strengthening the client's sense of self and supportive relationships. Writing measurable objectives helped me see the difference between a goal that sounds helpful and one that can actually be evaluated.

I considered CBT interventions for anxious thoughts and motivational interviewing for substance use. I did not want to assume that the client was ready to stop using marijuana before understanding what he believed it was doing for him. Treatment planning requires matching interventions to the client's needs, readiness, developmental stage, and priorities. The course also emphasized the connection between assessment, diagnosis, case conceptualization, intervention, documentation, and outcome monitoring. A plan should account for safety concerns, collaboration with other providers, referrals, and discharge criteria. Progress measures and clinical feedback should tell me when an approach is helping and when it needs to change.

A treatment plan needs to be specific enough that I can tell whether something is changing. At the same time, I do not want a client to feel like they are being reduced to a set of objectives. If the plan no longer reflects what the client needs, then the plan should change. The client should not have to fit the paperwork.

Couples and Family

Understanding addiction in the context of family systems and relationships.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTImpact of Gambling Disorder on Family Functioning
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

My paper on gambling disorder focused on what happens to a family when one person's gambling becomes a recurring problem. The financial consequences are serious, but I was also interested in secrecy, broken trust, and the changes family members make in response. A partner may start monitoring money or taking over responsibilities, and those responses can become part of the family's everyday functioning.

Family systems theory helped me look at these recurring interactions without treating them as evidence that everyone is equally responsible. I think that distinction matters. Understanding how a pattern continues is not the same as excusing the behavior that caused harm. Roles, boundaries, communication, and family expectations all help explain what happens between people.

The course introduced different approaches to couples and family counseling, including assessment of relationship patterns and ways to establish shared treatment goals. I also had to consider how family structure, culture, religion, and expectations about responsibility affect what each person wants from counseling. A strategy that makes sense to one family may not fit another. Working with several people also changes the ethical responsibilities of the counselor. I would need to clarify confidentiality, informed consent, and my role from the beginning, especially when family members have competing goals. I also cannot assume that joint sessions are appropriate without assessing safety and the possibility of coercion or other harm.

Although I am interested in individual counseling, I do not think individuals can be understood entirely apart from their relationships. Family members can provide support, but they can also be affected by the same problems a client is trying to address. This course helped me think about what change will look like once the client leaves the counseling room.

Diagnostic

Case formulation and distinguishing clinical interpretation from assumptions.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTCase Conceptualization Paper
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

I see diagnosis as necessary, but I do not think naming a disorder is the same as understanding the person who has it. The DSM-5-TR helps establish whether symptoms meet criteria for a disorder, including duration, impairment, and other diagnostic requirements. It cannot, by itself, explain the meaning someone attaches to their experiences.

In my case conceptualization of Ted Kaczynski, I used an existential perspective to consider isolation, autonomy, and his understanding of the world. This was an exercise in conceptualization, not a substitute for a direct clinical assessment. I had to distinguish what the available material supported from what I was interpreting through a theoretical lens. That distinction is particularly important when discussing a person who has not been evaluated in a counseling setting.

Coursework in psychopathology strengthened my understanding of mood, anxiety, trauma-related, psychotic, personality, and substance-related disorders. Differential diagnosis requires more than matching a symptom to a category. Substance use, medical conditions, medications, developmental history, and cultural context can all change how a presentation should be understood. I have also learned the value of a thorough clinical interview, mental status examination, screening, and risk assessment. Sometimes the most responsible conclusion is that more information is needed. I would rather document uncertainty and continue assessing than force a presentation into a diagnosis before I can support it.

My existential and humanistic interests influence how I understand clients, but they do not replace diagnostic competence. I want to be accurate about symptoms and impairment while still asking what a person's experiences mean to them. Both questions matter, and neither answers the other completely.

Addictions

Substance use prevention, treatment access, and program planning.

KEY SIGNATURE ARTIFACTChicago Youth Sobriety Center
Open PDF ↗
View assignment on this page
Competency Reflection

For our Addictions project, we developed the Chicago Youth Sobriety Center, a proposed treatment program for adolescents with problematic marijuana use. We had to think about the actual services a young person could access, how the program would operate, and what would make it sustainable. A treatment model may look good on paper, but it does not accomplish much if the people it is meant to serve cannot use it.

Addictions coursework covered the biological and neurological processes involved in substance use, diagnostic criteria, risk and protective factors, and the relationship between addiction and other mental health conditions. I learned to consider intoxication, withdrawal, patterns of use, and how substance use may affect functioning differently depending on a person's developmental stage. I also studied screening and assessment, levels of care, motivational interviewing, CBT, relapse prevention, and recovery support. A central question for me is what function the substance or behavior serves. If someone uses marijuana to manage anxiety, telling them to stop without helping them find another way to cope leaves an important part of the problem untouched.

My coursework on gambling disorder also showed me how behavioral addictions can affect finances, relationships, and daily functioning. Addiction treatment involves more than identifying the behavior. Counselors may need to work with families, coordinate referrals, address co-occurring conditions, and consider community resources that support recovery.

I am interested in motivational interviewing and harm reduction because they require me to understand where the client is rather than where I think they should be. If a person is not ready to stop using a substance, that does not mean there is nothing to work on. We can still examine consequences, build other coping skills, and discuss what change would realistically look like.

Trauma

Trauma, oppression, identity, and culturally responsive clinical care.

Competency Reflection

For my Slingshot Hip Hop paper, I examined Palestinian rappers living under occupation and the effects of ongoing violence on mental health. One of the central questions for me was what it means to diagnose distress when the conditions producing that distress are still present. PTSD symptoms may be clinically significant, but that does not mean the problem exists only within the individual. Sometimes the environment itself is unsafe.

Trauma coursework helped me understand PTSD, complex and repeated trauma, and the effects of traumatic stress on memory, emotion, relationships, and the nervous system. I learned that trauma can influence how someone experiences safety and control long after an event, and that ongoing exposure presents different challenges from processing a past event that has ended.

I studied trauma-informed care, stabilization, grounding, emotional regulation, and approaches including Trauma-Focused CBT and narrative interventions. My case paper considered how storytelling and music can help people express experiences that may otherwise be difficult to communicate. I have learned that treatment should be paced according to the client's stability and readiness rather than forcing trauma processing too early. The course also addressed cultural and historical trauma, grief, resilience, and the risk of retraumatization. Trauma does not affect everyone in the same way. I have to consider the person's age, environment, support system, and cultural understanding of what happened. Safety, choice, and collaboration are especially important when someone has experienced a loss of control.

I am interested in the connection between trauma and the body, and I hope to pursue training in somatic approaches. I also know that interest alone is not clinical competence. I need to keep learning established trauma treatments, assessment, stabilization, and how to judge whether a client is ready for deeper processing. My goal is to understand both what happened to someone and what they are still living through.
